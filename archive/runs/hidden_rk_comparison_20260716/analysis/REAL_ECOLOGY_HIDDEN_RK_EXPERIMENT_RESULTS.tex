\begin{abstract}
This report presents the matched comparison between the benchmark's
parameter-known and hidden-demographics regimes.  The former exposes the true
demographic parameters $r$ and $K$ and is retained as a known-parameter
ablation or upper-bound arm.  The latter hides $r$, $K$, demographic family,
table-derived action effects, and private safety quantities from every method;
it is the corrected structure-unknown uncertainty test.  Both arms contain
\ReportMatchedCells{} matched population/family/noise/reward cells and use the
same frozen code, data budget, planning budget, and evaluation seeds.  Hiding
demographics substantially changes the native-comparator result in safe mode:
the cellwise best general method changes from a mean gap of
\SafeFullMoorGap{} against MOOR-native in the full arm to
\SafeHiddenMoorGap{} in the hidden arm.  The safe-mode result must be read
with the pre-registered caveat that public safety channels are weak under the
private safety objective.
\end{abstract}

\section{Summary}

The analyzed artifacts are under the dated run directory listed in
Table~\ref{tab:setting}.  This is a fresh, current-semantics comparison between
two deliberately distinct information regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full} (\texttt{expose\_rk=full}): known $r,K$,
    public-demographics, parameter-known.  This is the retained comparison
    arm and an informative upper-bound ablation; it is not discarded or
    characterized as erroneous.
    \item \textbf{Hidden} (\texttt{expose\_rk=hidden}): hidden demographics,
    structure unknown.  Methods receive sanitized public transitions, action
    costs, and opaque population identity, while the evaluator privately keeps
    the true dynamics and safety objective.
\end{itemize}

The principal findings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Both arms completed all \ReportRowsPerRegime{} method rows.  Their
    \ReportMatchedCells{} scientific cells match exactly, with
    \ReportUnmatchedCells{} unmatched cells.
    \item In safe mode, the cellwise best general method is above MOOR-native
    in \SafeHiddenMoorWins{} hidden cells with mean gap
    \SafeHiddenMoorGap{}, and above PLUS-native in
    \SafeHiddenPlusWins{} cells with mean gap \SafeHiddenPlusGap{}.
    Under full information, both native methods instead have the higher mean.
    \item In yield mode, hidden native fitting closes much of the full-mode
    advantage but does not reverse it on average: the hidden best-general gaps
    are \YieldHiddenMoorGap{} against MOOR-native and
    \YieldHiddenPlusGap{} against PLUS-native.
    \item The native safe-return sensitivity to hiding demographics is much
    larger than for the general methods: matched hidden-minus-full deltas are
    \SafeMoorDelta{} for MOOR-native and \SafePlusDelta{} for PLUS-native,
    compared with \SafeOgsrlDelta{} for OGSRL.
    \item Public ecological termination is nearly absent
    (\TerminationEvents{} events in \TerminationTransitions{} transitions),
    and constant-risk fallback activates in \RiskFallbackCells{} safe cells.
    Safe-mode comparisons are therefore information-limited under the private
    safety objective, not evidence that OGSRL intrinsically fails at safety.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiment Setting and Regime Contract}

The two arms differ only in \texttt{expose\_rk}.  They share the frozen
configuration, the same \ReportPopulationCount{} populations, four demographic families, four
observation-noise levels, two reward modes, five methods, and evaluation
budget.  The headline methods are RefPlan, BA-MCTS, OGSRL, MOOR-native, and
PLUS-native.  ``Best general'' is the cellwise maximum over RefPlan, BA-MCTS,
and OGSRL; it is a descriptive upper envelope, not a single deployable policy.

In hidden mode, method-side inputs may contain public observations and
histories, action identifiers and costs, time/horizon context, genuine public
termination, and an opaque population token.  They may not contain true state,
true $r$ or $K$, $K_{\rm ref}$ or $K_{\rm eff}$, demographic family, exact
action-effect tables, the private safety threshold or margin, exact safety
labels, \texttt{initially\_unsafe}, or evaluator-only reward components.
MOOR-native fits one public model from sanitized transitions.  PLUS-native
fits a separate public solver for each existing closed-world family candidate;
the candidate set is unchanged and no new open-world family or $r/K$ candidate
axis is introduced.

interpreted as an intrinsic test of OGSRL's safety capability.  In particular,
OGSRL is retained as a headline method, and its result is reported without
post-hoc retuning or demotion.

\section{Mechanistic Interpretation}

The matched regime comparison identifies a reproducible change in method
ordering, but the following mechanisms are interpretations rather than a
causal decomposition.  The evidence is \emph{consistent with} several linked
effects of the information boundary.  Isolating any one effect would require
an additional registered ablation, such as varying the public transition
budget, separately perturbing fitted action effects, or changing the public
safety information while holding the rest of the pipeline fixed.

\subsection{Why native solvers are strong with known demographics}

In the full regime, MOOR-native and PLUS-native receive exact demographic and
action-table information, including the population capacity scale and
action-specific growth effects used to construct their mechanistic solvers.
That access removes much of the parameter-estimation problem.  Their strong
full-mode returns, and the negative named-method gaps in
Figures~\ref{fig:general-method-family-heatmap} and
\ref{fig:general-method-sigma-heatmap}, are consistent with near-oracle
demographic inputs being particularly valuable to these native planning
models.  This remains a valid parameter-known result rather than an error in
the old arm.

\subsection{Why hidden safe mode changes the comparison}

In hidden mode, the native solvers must fit dynamics and action effects from
sanitized public transitions.  They cannot read the true $r$, $K$, family,
action-effect tables, or the $K$-scaled safety threshold.  One plausible
explanation for their safe-mode drop is accumulated estimation error in the
fitted ecological transition and action-response model, coupled with a safety
objective whose exact boundary remains evaluator-only.  Diagnostic evidence
indicates that the public reward surrogate is less accurate in the private
penalty-applied stratum than outside it (Table~\ref{tab:safety-diagnostics}),
so the available data do not expose the safe objective cleanly.

General methods may be less dependent on recovering a single exact ecological
parameterization.  Their learned return, transition, belief, and planning
patterns can exploit regularities in observed public trajectories even when
the demographic fit is imperfect.  This is consistent with the positive
hidden safe margins, particularly for OGSRL, but it is not proof that a
particular representation or planner causes the advantage.  The winner
composition in Figure~\ref{fig:general-winner-composition} also shows that the
safe envelope is predominantly OGSRL rather than a uniform three-method gain.

\subsection{Why the result does not make ecological structure useless}

Hidden yield mode provides an important counterweight.  The native solvers
remain competitive and are slightly better on average than the cellwise
best-general envelope; each named general method has a negative mean margin in
Table~\ref{tab:hidden-general-methods}.  This suggests that fitted ecological
structure can remain useful when the objective aligns more directly with
observable abundance and return, even without exact table access.  The result
is therefore about the interaction between information, objective, and method,
not a general rejection of mechanistic modeling.

\subsection{OGSRL and the weak public safety signal}

OGSRL's hidden safe result needs special caution.  Genuine ecological
termination occurs only \TerminationEvents{} times in
\TerminationTransitions{} public transitions, and constant-risk fallback is
used in \RiskFallbackCells{} safe cells.  Its learned public risk channel is
therefore nearly constant in most cells.  The observed safe return is
consistent with other public reward/model/planning signals carrying useful
information, but the experiment cannot separate those channels cleanly.  It
does not establish intrinsic OGSRL safety competence or failure under a
well-observed safety target.

\subsection{Role of the registered experimental scale}

The observed ordering is conditional on the registered scale: a 4,000-row
transition target with complete 25-step episodes; planner horizon 5, 96
candidate sequences, and 32 particles; five evaluation seeds with four
episodes per seed at horizon 50; observation noise from 0.0 to 0.4; and safe
collapse penalty $P=5$.  Recoverable and sink populations contribute very
different return distributions, as Figure~\ref{fig:hidden-general-gap-distributions}
shows.  A larger data budget could improve native parameter recovery; a longer
planner horizon or different search budget could change all methods; and a
different penalty could change the safe-return tradeoff.  None of these
counterfactuals was tuned after observing the hidden results, so they should be
treated as future ablations rather than explanations already demonstrated by
this run.

\section{Registered Interpretation and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The hidden arm is the corrected hidden-demographics,
    structure-unknown setting.  The full arm remains runnable and scientifically
    useful as the known-parameter comparison.
    \item Data collection targets 4,000 rows while preserving complete
    episodes.  The observed bounded overshoot is recorded rather than
    truncated away or used to increase the budget.
    \item The private safety threshold remains evaluator-only.  No public
    threshold was invented after observing results, and no method was retuned
    against hidden outcomes.
    \item Public low-reward-tail error and private safe/unsafe-stratified
    reward error are reporting diagnostics only.  They did not affect model
    selection, hyperparameters, or the headline grid.
    \item Safe mode is pre-registered as information-limited if both public
    safety channels are thin.  That condition is observed here and must
    accompany interpretation of safe-mode gaps.
    \item The prior 721-cell prevalence calculation included duplicated
    trajectories across penalty values and is not an independent replication.
    It is not used as independent evidence in this report; the risk conclusion
    is supported by the complete matched sweep reported here.
    \item Cellwise best-general results benefit from selecting the strongest
    of three general methods separately in each cell.  Per-method means and
    the all-method hidden figure are provided to prevent that descriptive
    envelope from being mistaken for a deployable selector.
    \item Illustrative rollout plots refit policies because serialized trained
    policies were not part of the headline artifacts.  They reproduce the
    registered fitting path and fixed seeds, but they are not additional
    headline experiments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The matched run closes the central information-routing question.  Exact
demographic access gives the native solvers a clear advantage in the retained
full arm.  Once all methods must infer dynamics from sanitized public data,
the safe-mode ordering reverses broadly across families, noise levels, and
recoverability scopes, while native structure remains competitive in yield
mode.  The comparison therefore supports treating known-$r,K$ and hidden-$r,K$
as separate benchmark regimes rather than merging their claims.

The safety result is narrower.  Because the evaluator's safety threshold is
private and genuine termination is exceptionally rare, public risk learning is
nearly inert and reward-surrogate error is larger in the private penalty
stratum.  Safe-mode outcomes should consequently be reported as performance
under limited public safety information, not as a standalone judgment of any
method's intrinsic safety mechanism.
